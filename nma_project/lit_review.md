1. Laquitaine, S. & Gardner, J.L. (2018). A switching observer for human perceptual estimation. Neuron 97(2):462-474. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2017.12.011
Finding: proposed a model where subjects switch between reporting the raw sensory measurement or the prior on individual trials, rather than continuously integrating both — this switching model explained subject data (including non-Gaussian/bimodal-leaning report distributions) better than a standard integrating Bayesian observer.

2. Chambers et al. (2018). The development of Bayesian integration in sensorimotor estimation. Journal of Vision 18(12):8. https://doi.org/10.1167/18.12.8
Finding (direct quote from the abstract/text found): "a Bayesian model predicted estimation behavior better than models which used only one source of information (prior or likelihood) or switched between prior and likelihood (Laquitaine & Gardner, 2018)" — i.e., in their task, full integration beat a switching model, in contrast to Laquitaine & Gardner's own conclusion

3. Landy, M.S. & Kojima, H. (2001). Ideal cue combination for localizing texture-defined edges. Journal of the Optical Society of America A 18(9):2307-2320. https://opg.optica.org/josaa/abstract.cfm?uri=josaa-18-9-2307
earlier switching-behavior precedent as it showed that human observers sometimes use one information source at a time rather than continuously combining both.. Finding: Two experiments required observers to localize texture-defined edges using two cues (disparity and texture). The most accurate estimates require combining all available information optimally. Critically, they found evidence that observers did not always integrate cues in a statistically optimal (weighted-average) fashion — instead, in some conditions observers appeared to select or switch between cues rather than blending them, an early precedent for the switching-vs-integration distinction that Laquitaine & Gardner (2018) later built on. 

4. Ernst, M.O. & Banks, M.S. (2002). Humans integrate visual and haptic information in a statistically optimal fashion. Nature 415:429-433. https://www.nature.com/articles/415429a
Finding: the nervous system combines two information sources by weighting each according to its reliability (inverse variance), closely matching maximum-likelihood-estimation predictions — the canonical basis for why any blending model (no switching required) predicts more prior-influence when sensory reliability (here, motion_coherence) drops.

5. Powers, A.R., Mathys, C. & Corlett, P.R. (2017). Pavlovian conditioning-induced hallucinations result from overweighting of perceptual priors. Science 357(6351):596-600. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aan3458
Finding: hallucinating subjects (with or without psychosis) were more susceptible to conditioning-induced hallucinations than non-hallucinators; a Bayesian model fit to their behavior showed increased weighting of perceptual priors relative to sensory evidence in those who hallucinate.

6. Adams, R.A., Stephan, K.E., Brown, H.R., Frith, C.D. & Friston, K.J. (2013). The computational anatomy of psychosis. Frontiers in Psychiatry 4:47. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2013.00047
Finding: frames psychotic symptoms as false Bayesian inference; proposes psychotic pathology corresponds to a reduction in the precision of prediction errors relative to prior beliefs, formalized via predictive-coding simulations (perceptual synthesis, smooth pursuit, agency attribution).

7. Corlett, P.R., Horga, G., Fletcher, P.C., Alderson-Day, B., Schmack, K. & Powers, A.R. (2019). Hallucinations and Strong Priors. Trends in Cognitive Sciences 23(2):114-127. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1364661318302821 
Finding (balancing view — read this to avoid overclaiming): explicitly reconciles two competing accounts — hallucinations linked to strong, overly precise priors dominating perception vs. a separate literature on weak/imprecise priors in psychosis — and argues both can be true depending on which level of the inference hierarchy is affected. 

8. Cassidy, C.M., et al. (2018). A perceptual inference mechanism for hallucinations linked to striatal dopamine. Current Biology 28(4):503-514. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2017.12.059
Finding: Studied unmedicated schizophrenia patients with varying hallucination severity using a novel auditory task (VCTR — variable context tone reproduction) that manipulates prior expectations and their uncertainty. Hallucination severity correlated with a perceptual bias reflecting disproportional gain on prior expectations under uncertainty — i.e., patients with worse hallucinations relied more on priors even when those priors were imprecise (high variance), failing to appropriately down-weight them. This bias could be pharmacologically induced by amphetamine in healthy subjects, strongly correlated with striatal dopamine release (measured via PET), and was related to cortical volume of the dorsal anterior cingulate (a region tracking environmental uncertainty). The findings outline a dopamine-dependent gain-control mechanism where excessive prior weighting under uncertainty confers vulnerability to hallucinations (SQ4)
